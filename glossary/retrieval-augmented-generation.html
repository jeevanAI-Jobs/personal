What Is Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)?

A plain-language definition of Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), how it works, and what it means for which brands and sources appear in AI responses.

Definition

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is an AI architecture that combines a large language model with a real-time document retrieval step: before generating a response, the system searches a document store or the web for relevant passages, then uses those retrieved passages as grounding context for the generated answer.

RAG in Plain Terms

Before RAG, language models answered questions from memory: they generated text based entirely on what they learned during training. The limitation is obvious: training data has a cutoff date, and models cannot retrieve specific documents or cite current sources.

RAG adds a lookup step. When a question arrives, the system first searches a document index or the live web, retrieves the most relevant passages, and then hands those passages to the language model as context for generating the answer. The model uses those retrieved passages to ground its response, which is why RAG-powered systems can cite sources and produce more specific, current answers.

Perplexity is a prominent example of a consumer-facing RAG system. ChatGPT with web browsing enabled uses a similar approach. Many enterprise AI applications run internal RAG over proprietary document stores.

How RAG Affects Which Brands Get Cited

In a RAG system, the retrieved documents determine what the model has to work with. If your brand's content is not retrieved, the model cannot cite it. This creates a two-step funnel for brand visibility:

  1. Retrieval eligibility: Is your content indexed and retrievable by the system's search step? This depends on crawlability, freshness, and the system's document sources.
  2. Passage relevance: Once retrieved, does your content contain clear, specific, extractable answers that the model can use? Vague or unfocused content may be retrieved but not surfaced in the response.

For brands, this means that traditional SEO thinking (ranking a page highly) maps partially onto RAG optimization (making your content the most relevant retrieved document for category queries). The key difference is that RAG systems may retrieve from sources beyond your own domain, including review sites, forums, and third-party publications.

Why RAG Matters for AI Visibility Strategy

Many of the AI systems buyers use for product research are either fully RAG-based or use retrieval as a grounding layer. Understanding RAG shifts the strategic frame: the question is not just "does the model know about my brand from training?" but "is my brand's content being retrieved and used to ground responses?"

This makes ongoing content quality, structured formatting, and presence on cited source platforms (review sites, industry directories, news sites) directly relevant to visibility. For a closer look at how retrieval-based AI systems select what to cite, see the RAG and brand visibility guide.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does RAG stand for?

RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation. It describes an AI architecture in which a language model is combined with a document retrieval step that grounds the generated response in current, specific sources.

Which AI systems use RAG?

Perplexity is built around RAG. ChatGPT with web browsing enabled uses a similar retrieval approach. Many enterprise AI applications run RAG over internal document stores. Google AI Overviews also incorporate retrieval from the web.

Does having a good website help with RAG-based AI visibility?

Yes. If your site is crawled by the systems those AI platforms use for retrieval, well-structured content with clear, specific answers to category questions is more likely to be retrieved and cited than vague or poorly organized pages.

What is the difference between RAG and standard ChatGPT?

Standard ChatGPT (without browsing) generates responses from training data alone, with a knowledge cutoff. ChatGPT with browsing enabled adds a retrieval step that fetches live web pages to ground its responses, making it more RAG-like.
